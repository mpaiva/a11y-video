Testing the home page with VoiceOver

A first pass at testing this site's home page with VoiceOver, the screen reader built into the Mac, before testing it with people with disabilities. The goal was to find barriers early and fix them. Each step is a short video; each problem found was fixed and tested again.

The brief

The home page works with a keyboard and a screen reader for most of what was tested. It has a skip link, a clear outline of headings, named regions, and controls that say what they are and what state they are in. Focus is always visible, and Escape always puts it back where you were.

The test found five problems on the page. Three are fixed and live, one fix is in review, and one, a small one, is still open. One more thing heard was VoiceOver and Safari, not the page.

  1. Fixed A hidden menu held VoiceOver for four presses

    Just after the Set for you button, VoiceOver said “menu” four times and didn’t move. There was no menu on screen: the closed settings panel still held its four dropdowns, and Safari passed them to VoiceOver. Someone listening would think the page had stopped.

    Fixed: the panel is now left empty while it is closed. Live since October 16, 2026.

    WCAG 4.1.2 Name, Role, Value · Watch it

  2. Fixed Changing a setting wasn’t announced

    Turning the drawings off worked, but nothing said so. The note confirming it was only on screen, so someone listening had to go and look for it.

    Fixed: a status line now says what each change did. VoiceOver says “Drawings are off and their captions go with them.” Live since October 17, 2026.

    WCAG 4.1.3 Status Messages · Watch it

  3. Fix in review Describe this page opened in silence

    The button that describes the page’s layout in words opened its dialog without VoiceOver saying anything. Someone listening wouldn’t know it had opened, though what it says is useful once you find it.

    A fix makes the dialog read its title and first sentences when it opens. It was heard working, and is in review, not yet live.

    Not a WCAG failure on its own; it stops the feature doing its job · Watch it

  4. Fixed Essay links in the drawing strip read their title twice

    In the band of drawings, each link read its essay’s title twice, then the whole summary. The links made sense, but a list of them was slow to get through.

    Fixed: each link now says its title once, then the summary. Live since October 16, 2026.

    WCAG 2.4.4 Link Purpose is met; a listening cost · Watch it

  5. Open The strip’s preview is read again after its links

    Reading the page item by item, the preview card for the drawing in the middle of the strip is read after the strip’s links, so one essay’s title and summary are heard a third time.

    Open: hiding the preview from screen readers would stop it, while its summary still reaches the link. Not done yet.

    No WCAG failure; a listening cost ·

  6. Not the page Choosing from a dropdown reads the old value first

    When you pick a new value in a dropdown, VoiceOver first reads the value it had before. This happens with Safari’s standard dropdown, not because of the page; the status line now follows it with the right answer.

    VoiceOver in Safari · Watch it

An earlier draft. This is the first, one-narrator version of the test, kept as it was made. The two-voice version is the current one: it adds the fixes made since, and corrects this draft’s Describe this page finding. The dialog did not open in silence; that came from the test runner, not the page.

What these videos are. VoiceOver in Safari, on macOS 27, driven by a script so each test can be run again the same way. What you hear from VoiceOver is what it said. The narrator is a synthetic voice, describing what a tester listens for.

What they are not. An audit, or a claim that the site conforms to WCAG. It is one specialist, one screen reader and one browser, on one page. Nobody who uses a screen reader every day took part: that comes next, in the recommendations.

Captions. Every video has captions for both voices on screen. They are also in each video as a captions track, and as a file you can download, with a transcript under each video.

How it was tested

  1. Inspect the page structure before using the screen reader. Goal: understand the intended structure and find obvious semantic problems. Video
  2. Test keyboard-only navigation. Goal: the page works without a mouse, before the screen reader comes in. Video
  3. A linear reading pass with the screen reader. Goal: the experience of discovering and understanding the page. Video
  4. Structural navigation: headings, landmarks, links. Goal: finding information without reading the whole page. Video
  5. Controls and interactive components. Goal: controls are understandable and usable, not merely discoverable. Video
  6. Real user tasks, start to finish. Goal: people can complete what they came to do. Video

  33. 2:30 Narrator That's the end of the first pass. Five problems found on the page: three fixed and live, one fix in review, and one still open. The next test is with people.
  1. Describe this page. Whether a written description of the layout actually helps someone build a picture of the page, whether it says too much or too little, and whether people find the button where it is. A screen reader can show the dialog works; only the people it is for can say it helps. Who: blind people who use a screen reader every day, on VoiceOver and on JAWS or NVDA.
  2. Other screen readers and devices. This pass used VoiceOver in Safari only, and its biggest finding came from how Safari passes hidden content to VoiceOver. JAWS and NVDA on Windows, VoiceOver on iPhone and TalkBack on Android behave differently. Who: people who use each of them daily, on their own devices and settings.
  3. Set for you, as a sentence of settings. The panel reads its settings as a sentence, with a dropdown in each part. Whether that reads naturally, and whether the new announcements are enough to know what changed, is a question of experience, not of code. Who: screen-reader users, and people with cognitive or learning disabilities.
  4. Motion. The band of drawings drifts and the background moves. The pause button and reduced-motion setting were checked to work, not whether they are found in time or whether the motion is comfortable before they are. Who: people with vestibular disorders, and people for whom motion makes reading harder, such as some people with ADHD.
  5. Zoom and magnification. The header stays at the top of the screen, and some text sits over moving colour that the site measures but doesn’t yet grade. How that feels at high zoom or under a screen magnifier wasn’t part of this pass. Who: people with low vision who use browser zoom or a screen magnifier.
  6. Voice control and switch access. Controls were checked for names a screen reader announces, not for names people would say aloud, and the home page has more than fifty stops from top to bottom for someone moving one step at a time. Who: people who use Voice Control or switch access, and others with motor disabilities.
  7. The collage layout. Set for you can swap the home page’s list of essays for a collage of drawings. This pass tested the list only. Who: screen-reader and keyboard users.
